# Niche: anime action

KIND: film

A drawn action scene of 30-90 s. Read `film` first: the cast, the place, how shots join and the checks are the same.
What follows is only what action adds, for any fight and any fighters. The evidence is in `film`'s `LAB.md`
(experiments 29-50). A lesson from a new action video goes here in general terms; one that holds for any film goes into `film`.

LOOK
- Calm shots: "a shot from a hand-drawn 2D anime action series, cel shading, bold ink lines, dramatic shadows, saturated
  colour, not photorealistic; no subtitles, no captions, no written text; no background music".
- Fighting: add "fast fluid sakuga animation with smear frames, impact frames that flash white and black, speed lines,
  sparks, a dynamic camera that follows the action".
- Quiet shots after the fight go back to the calm look: the fight wording puts an impact frame into them.

SHOT LANGUAGE (what makes it read as anime, not a stage play)
- Cut fast: 1.3-2.5 s a shot on the timeline. Ask for 2-3 s and cut the first 0.45 s off each shot made on its own.
- A scene told only in side-view two-shots is clear and looks like theatre. Mix:
  - eyes only, in an extreme close-up, one opponent then the other;
  - feet digging in; a dash straight at the camera; a weapon coming at the lens;
  - the weapons only, a flurry with afterimages; a clash seen from a low angle between the two;
  - both faces either side of the locked weapons; the struck person skidding back in dust;
  - a thing alone: the floor breaking, a projectile in flight ("no people in the picture").
- **Close action shots have no place**: "the background is abstract anime speed lines in <two colours>, not a place".
  Anime does this, and it removes the problem of the place changing between shots.
- Shots that show the place use the scene's one-sentence place.

THE FIGHTING ITSELF
- **A blow that lands is two fighters, whole in one frame, seen from the side, close together, in ONE exchange**: "they
  trade fast blows, a burst of sparks at every clash"; "their weapons lock, they push face to face"; "she strikes at
  his head, he ducks under it and drives his shoulder into her". Written as a close two-person shot ("he kicks him
  across the jaw") the two just stand.
- **One fighter in every shot is not a fight**: they pose and never touch.
- **Not several actions in a shot, and not three attackers at once**: the second half is dropped, the fighters scatter.
  "One against many" is the many coming one after another.
- **A duel is a chain of 3-4 clips that carry on** (`continue_from`), one exchange in each: the exchange / the lock /
  the counter that ends it. Cause and effect stay in the chain: the blow, then the struck one going down or sliding away
  is the next clip of the chain, never a shot made on its own.
- **Several duels in one exact place: a stage** (see `film`): the constant fighter at one side of a side view, the first
  clip of each duel starting on that frame with the opponent coming in from the empty side.
- Keep the two close enough to fill the frame. Written "far apart" both are tiny: bring one to the other first.
- A held pose with a delayed fall is not drawn (two people stand). A fall is a clip that carries on from the blow.
- **Blows that are drawn**: a strike to the body that knocks the other back; a strike that knocks the weapon out of
  the hand (it flies off, 2 of 2). A sweep of the legs was not drawn (0 of 1).
- **End a duel inside the frame**: "drops to one knee in front of her". A loser knocked out of the frame cannot be
  shown in the next clip of the chain; cut to a close shot of them instead, with their new state as a cast entry of
  its own (see `film`: a person whose state changes).
- A fighter without a weapon (fists, gauntlets) has nothing that can change between shots: the easiest opponent.

THE ORDER OF A DUEL (a pattern that held)
1. eyes of one, eyes of the other (speed lines)
2. the launch: feet, the dash at the camera
3. the weapon at the lens
4. the exchange, the lock, the counter (the side-view chain, cut to its flashes)
5. the struck one sliding or kneeling (the last clip of the chain)

WEAPONS
- In words, with limits, in the owner's description; where it is in every shot (see `film`, the cast).
- Small distinct weapons may be props with a picture; long thin ones are words only.
- A weapons-only shot (the flurry) names no fighter as a subject (see `film`: a shot of things only).
- A weapon thrust at the lens: "its narrow point comes at the lens". "The blade crosses the frame" drew a broad blade.

SOUND
- An impact frame is a flash: find the flashes in the picture (a scene-change score above 0.5 between two frames, at
  most one each 0.6 s) and put an impact (`sfx.make`) on each, with a whoosh ending on it.
- The model's own fight sound about 5 dB under the voices; one bed of the place's air under everything.
- Music in parts: a sparse tense track under the standoff, a fast one from the first blow (cut on that frame), faded
  under the last shots. One track for the whole scene stays at one level and reads as flat.

CHECKS (besides those of `film`)
- The cut check's line is 0.7 here (fast action measures 0.1-0.45; a cut to a reference picture 0.9-1.0). In the
  side-view chains the flashes are wanted: look at those by eye.
- Every action shot: is the action there, done by the right one, with the weapon in the hand?
- Across the cut: the weapon the same length and held the same way; the struck one where the blow would send them.

LIMITS (say them to the owner before starting)
- The motion does not match hand-animated fights of a studio. What can match is the shot language, the pace and the frames.
- About 20 s to make a 2-3 s shot at draft size; a fight of a minute is 30 shots and about 15 minutes with retakes.
